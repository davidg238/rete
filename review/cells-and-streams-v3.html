first, two things I got wrong

Retractions

v2 · “we are building etcd”

Overweight, withdrawn. That vocabulary was built for machines that are all awake and all talking. These are loosely coupled nodes, asleep most of the time, and nothing blocks on a request/response. Revision-consensus reasoning imported a difficulty that isn't here.

v3 draft · “notices vs letters”

Also wrong. There are no notices. Everything on the wire is a letter — a datagram saying setpoint is now 22. I had put the distinction in the mail. It doesn't live there.

What survives from v2 is one sentence, and it is the load-bearing one: the space has post and take, and no call. A job cannot ask another job for a value. Everything below follows from that plus the mailroom.

what this node actually is

Not a cluster. A building full of people who are mostly asleep.

the temperature node

Wakes every 15 min, reports, sleeps. Fire and forget. Never asks anyone anything.

the window sensor

A GPIO edge wakes it; it queues a change-of-status and goes back to sleep. Fire and forget.

the PID controller

Always on, driving a plant. Every 10 s it wakes the radio and checks its inbox for a setpoint or mode change.

Two of these three need nothing at all from this decision. They post and forget; the mailboy carries it. The PID is the entire argument — and, as we'll see, only because it crashes.

the mailroom, precisely

The mailboy never opens the letter

He reads what is written on the envelope — the address (goal/sp) and the sequence number. He does not, and need not, know a setpoint from a mode from an app manifest. That is the whole reason he can be given the widest reach in the building and still be harmless.

the mailboy, in full:
deliver: letter = (
  | path |
  path:: letter at: #path.               the address on the envelope
  (letter at: #dead ifAbsent: [ false ])
    ifTrue:  [ goal clear: path ]
    ifFalse: [ goal put: path value: (letter at: #value) ] )
                                         ↑ #value is never looked INSIDE

The assistant opens it. The PID job takes the record, reads 22 out of it, and adjusts. That is application code, and it is the only code in the node that knows what a setpoint means.

so where IS the difference?

In the pigeonhole. Not in the mail.

a stream — a pigeonhole you empty

Letters queue up. Taking one removes it. Each is delivered once. If nobody is in the office, it is dropped (next slide).

tel/print · sys/panic · goal/do/reboot · dbg/req

a cell — a pigeonhole that holds the latest

Holds the most recent letter only. Reading does not remove it. A new letter replaces the old. You may look in it any time — including for the first time, ten minutes after it arrived.

goal/sp · goal/mode · goal/apps/<n> · obs/health

Every pigeonhole in the space today is the first kind. That is the entire gap — not a new concept, not a second naming system: a second behaviour for a slot in the rack we already have.

verified in src/space.c, not assumed

Three facts that decide this

factwhereconsequence
every name is a queue space_post / space_take / space_register no slot holds a value. Nothing to look in.
an event with nobody listening is DROPPED, not held space_dequeue snapshots the reaction list at drain time, then queue[idx].live = 0 unconditionally (space.c:262) a job that was down when the letter arrived gets nothing when it comes back. The stream does not wait for you.
there is no request/reply there is post and take; there is no call a restarted job cannot ask the mailboy, or anyone, to re-send.

Those three together: after a crash, the PID's setpoint is unrecoverable from anything that exists today. Not slow to recover — unrecoverable, without going back to porta over a radio that may be unreachable.

interaction · 1 of 4 · normal delivery

A setpoint arrives

Nothing surprising, and nothing in PidJobA mentions a radio, a bridge, or porta. It holds a goal capability and that is all it knows about the world.

the only reason a cell exists

The office burns down

The PID crashes. That is not an exception here — it is doctrine. Let-it-crash, the supervisor rebuilds it, Milestone D exists to make this routine.

So: the assistant is new, and the desk is empty. His predecessor's note saying 22 burned with the office. And:

  • he cannot phone the mailroom — there is no request/reply;
  • the letter is gone — it was drained and dropped while he was dead;
  • and porta may be unreachable, out of range, or asleep — and even if it isn't, a network round trip must never be on the read path.

So the question is exactly this and nothing more: is there a pigeonhole he can walk over and look in?

retraction · david, 2026-07-13

There is no clear:. Stop is a value, not an absence.

“In industrial control there is no clear. There is a start button and a separate stop button — and stop always wins, even if both are pressed.”

An earlier draft of this slide argued for a clear: verb and a tombstone, on the grounds that the operator un-deployed blink and nobody ever mentioned blink must be distinguishable. They must — but deletion is the wrong way to say it.

running

auto = true · runlevel: #running. An explicit value.

stopped

auto = false · runlevel: #stopped. Also an explicit value — not a hole where a value used to be.

never mentioned

peek:ifAbsent: takes the else-branch → the fail-safe default, which is what you wanted anyway.

So a cell is value, and nothing else. No tombstone. No clear:. Three prims, not four. And tuvm#21 comes off the critical path — a cell needs two states, not three, and the kind (never written / is a cell) answers it without touching take:'s nil-vs-empty bug at all.

If a record must ever be removed — not stopped, removed — that is an operator action: a letter on a do/ stream. Not a property of the pigeonhole.

the decision, as small as it goes

Whose wall is the rack on?

A · the company's wall

The rack is in space.c: a value slot, a seq, a tombstone flag. Four prims. Cells outlive every job, including the mailboy.

recommended

B · a rack somebody else answers for

The space learns this prefix is answered by that object — Plan 9's move. Cells become ordinary nsl. Drivers could serve dev/** too.

later, and A becomes its first server

C · inside the mailboy's bag

An nsl Map in the bridge. No VM change — and the seven forced lines, plus the one crash that wipes the node's desired state.

not this

A and B are indistinguishable to every job. goal peek: 'sp' either way; same capability, same grant. They differ only in who implements the answer — and B can be added later without touching a line of job code, because A's cell simply becomes B's first server.

the path — and the one test that settles it

Rung 9, and its gate

  1. A value slot in space.c — that is the whole of it. The verb declares the kind: put:value: makes a cell, out:value: makes a stream, and the wrong verb on an existing name is an error (EISDIR, and nobody has ever called that a design flaw).
  2. Three prims — spaceKind: · spacePeek: · spacePut:value: — and two thin Channel methods: put:value: and peek:ifAbsent:.
  3. put posts onto the existing ring, so a whenever: on a cell fires through the unchanged reactor. No second delivery mechanism. The only difference from a post is that the cell also kept a copy.
  4. The cell's value is a GC root. It is reachable from nothing else in the heap — that is what a cell is. Its own test, because the failure mode would be an occasional wrong setpoint on a running plant.
  5. The gate — host only, no radio, no bridge, no porta:

Write a setpoint · crash the job · let the supervisor restart it · the job reads the correct setpoint back.
That test passes under A. It cannot be written under C — which is the argument, executable, in about forty lines.

And B stays unbuilt and unblocked. The radio is a driver; dev/** can become served prefixes the day drivers want it; nothing here forecloses it.
